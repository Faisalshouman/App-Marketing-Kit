# Hooks and loops

Choose the visual first. Then write the shortest line that gives viewers a reason to look or listen.

| Viewer action | Opening pattern | Example | Required payoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Look for something | Participation | Show ambiguous ink patches. “What do you see?” | Reveal the subject and return to the same image. |
| Understand the cause | Result first | Open on the catch. “A tower caught a spaceship.” | Replay the contact and show which parts made it possible. |
| Follow a detail | Visual cue | Start just before the jump. “Watch the wheels.” | Keep the move and landing legible in one shot. |
| Make a prediction | Prediction | Show two plausible outcomes in one frame. “Where will it land?” | Resolve the choice clearly with the observed result. |
| Reconcile a contradiction | Contrast | Show the motion. “What’s moving that tiny fin?” | Identify the real mechanism and give a visual clue or evidence. |
| See what changes | Before and after | Keep both states in the same crop while the star's glare drops. | Reveal the planet and clarify what the image or diagram depicts. |
| See a human result | Human payoff | Show an avatar speaking. “Watch what happens when she tries to speak.” | Connect the visible action to the actual attempted-speech system. |

A curiosity gap works best as a concrete missing piece. Start with a question, a result with a missing cause, or a visual that invites a prediction. For a result-first cut, give a second payoff by showing a detail or explaining the mechanism. Let the footage show what's surprising before giving it a label.

## Select the replay join

- **Spoken.** Write the ending and opening as one natural sentence. Read the opening on its own, then read across the join. For example, end on “And that’s how” and restart on “a tower caught a spaceship.” Record the joined phrase as one take when the workflow allows it. Make room for the cut without adding a conspicuous pause or sound reset.
- **Visual.** Return to the opening crop and focal position, or match a moving object’s direction and speed across the join. A reveal that changes how the opening reads gives the viewer a reason to look again. For moving loops, respect the frame cadence and check for a repeated frame or sudden jump.
- **Clean ending.** Hold the evidence or resolve the story when a join would make it harder to follow. Keep an end card out of a seamless join if it breaks continuity.

Give the viewer a shareable experience or fact. A visual puzzle lets a friend try it. A surprising result gives someone concrete evidence to show. Keep the title searchable and accurate. For a puzzle, preserve the surprise in the title or cover when that is part of the experience.

## Evidence behind the choices

- [YouTube’s conversation with Jenny Hoyos and Todd Sherman](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shorts-deep-dive/) gives a creator’s approach to first-frame clarity and quick satisfaction.
- [Loewenstein’s psychology of curiosity](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75) describes wanting to fill a recognized knowledge gap.
- [Berger and Milkman’s research on online sharing](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353) examines sharing in other media. Treat surprise and awe as creative hypotheses for short videos. Treat runtime, cut cadence, and replay effects as choices to test.
