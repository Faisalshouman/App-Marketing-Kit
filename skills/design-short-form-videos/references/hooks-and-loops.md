# Hooks and loops

Start with the payoff the source can actually deliver. Choose the opening frame or audio beat that makes the viewer's reason to continue clear. Write the shortest line that adds context or a question the image alone leaves open.

| Viewer action | Opening pattern | Example | Required payoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Look for something | Participation | Show ambiguous ink patches. “What do you see?” | Reveal the subject and return to the same image. |
| Understand the cause | Result first | Open on the catch. “A tower caught a spaceship.” | Replay the contact and show which parts made it possible. |
| Follow a detail | Visual cue | Start just before the jump. “Watch the wheels.” | Keep the move and landing legible in one shot. |
| Make a prediction | Prediction | Show two plausible outcomes in one frame. “Where will it land?” | Resolve the choice clearly with the observed result. |
| Reconcile a contradiction | Contrast | Show the motion. “What’s moving that tiny fin?” | Identify the real mechanism and give a visual clue or evidence. |
| See what changes | Before and after | Keep both states in the same crop while the star's glare drops. | Reveal the planet and clarify what the image or diagram depicts. |
| See a human result | Human payoff | Show an avatar speaking. “Watch what happens when she tries to speak.” | Connect the visible action to the actual attempted-speech system. |
| Find the cause | Unexpected result | Show a real failed build beside the changed setting. | Reproduce the result and show what changed, including other plausible causes if the evidence leaves them open. |
| Apply an idea | Personal relevance | Show a familiar problem, such as recognizing a paragraph but being unable to explain it. | Demonstrate a practical action tied to the problem. Keep the implication within the evidence. |
| Compare two outcomes | Matched comparison | Show two model outputs from the same prompt and label the changed condition. | Point to the meaningful difference and disclose any uncontrolled variables. |
| Make sense of a claim | Evidence first | Open on the relevant panel of a study figure or the decisive step of a demo. | Explain what the measurement represents and keep the main qualification visible. |
| Understand an event | Immediate consequence | Start near a landing in a gameplay clip and show the goal or stakes. | Preserve the action-to-consequence order and enough setup to make the result intelligible. |

Choose the opening by what the viewer will do. Use a specific question for an answerable topic, a prediction when the audience has enough information to guess, evidence first for an immediately legible result, or an action cue when timing carries the story. For contradiction, state the evidenced contrast and name a familiar belief only when there is reason to think the audience holds it. For a podcast, retain the question, qualifier or setup if removing it changes what the guest meant. When comparing openings is worthwhile, try direct result, specific question and prediction where they fit the same payoff. Ask first-time viewers what they expect to see before revealing the answer.

## Choose what the loop does

| Pattern | When to use it | How to finish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question and answer | The viewer can understand one worthwhile question. | Resolve it explicitly on the first play. A replay join is optional. |
| Prediction and reveal | Two plausible outcomes can be understood at a glance or from a short setup. | Give a readable beat to choose, reveal the result, and explain it. Keep the answer accessible without guessing correctly. |
| Recontextualization | A later fact genuinely changes the meaning of the opening. | Explain the fact, then return to the opening with that context. The return can happen within the cut. |
| Rewatchable clue | An early detail is already visible and actually helps explain the outcome. | Explain the clue after completing the payoff. Return attention to it so the viewer can recognize it sooner or interpret it differently. |
| Visual replay join | The subject, framing or motion can carry naturally across the boundary. | Plan the last and first moments together. For a still match, align crop, subject, exposure and overlay state. For motion, match direction, speed and cadence across adjacent frames. |
| Spoken replay join | The ending naturally leads into the first line, with the answer already delivered. | Read the opening alone and both fragments in sequence. Record the joined phrase in one take where practical. Give theme, speech and ambient sound enough room to join cleanly. |
| Clean ending | A recap, final proof, joke, qualified statement or CTA benefits from a clear stop. | Finish on a legible answer or action. Keep this as the comparison when testing whether a replay treatment has value. |

Question-and-answer and prediction loops describe how the story closes. A replay join describes how playback restarts. For an intentional replay, record what the second play adds in one sentence. If the only reason to replay is that the first pass was too fast or left out the answer, improve the first pass.

For technical execution, choose the picture, spoken and sound treatments in [Replay construction](replay-construction.md). A seamless boundary and a worthwhile second play are separate decisions. Use the story's meaningful transitions inside the cut, even when the last-to-first join is smooth.

## Build the boundary

1. Mark the payoff beat and verify the ending still leaves time to see or hear it at normal speed. Mark the last and first frames against the delivery timeline and record the audio, caption and overlay state at both.
2. Preserve temporal continuity. For moving joins, check the last few and first few decoded frames at the delivery frame rate. A circular animation usually omits an endpoint that repeats frame zero. A static match can intentionally hold the same frame. For an intentional hard cut, check that the action and direction still read.
3. Listen for changes in room tone, a clipped syllable, a beat reset or a click. Match ambience or use a short crossfade where it preserves speech. Check final audio against the video boundary and caption cues. Watch two consecutive plays at phone size, with sound and muted where applicable.

### Worked example

For a short on curiosity and memory, open on an illustrative trivia prompt and ask which answers participants later recalled better, the ones they were more or less curious about. Reveal that recall was better for the answers rated higher for curiosity. Explain how the researchers grouped trials by those ratings. If you return to the prompt, a viewer should now understand why the curiosity rating sits between the question and the reveal. Use a clean ending if returning to the prompt adds little. [Gruber et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/)

Give the viewer a shareable experience or fact. A visual puzzle lets a friend try it. A surprising result gives someone concrete evidence to show. Keep the title searchable and accurate. For a puzzle, preserve the surprise in the title or cover when that is part of the experience.

## Evidence behind the choices

- [YouTube’s conversation with Jenny Hoyos and Todd Sherman](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shorts-deep-dive/) gives a creator’s approach to first-frame clarity and quick satisfaction.
- [Berger and Milkman’s research on online sharing](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353) examines sharing in other media. Treat surprise and awe as creative hypotheses for short videos. Treat runtime, cut cadence, and replay effects as choices to test.
